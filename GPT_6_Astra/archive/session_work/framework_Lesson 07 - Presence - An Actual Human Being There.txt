Hi, and welcome to Lesson 7. This lesson is about presence, which is the third of the four components of essential demand. In the last two lessons, we covered affinity, which is wanting a particular person, and accountability, which is needing someone on the hook. Presence is simpler than either of those, and in some ways it runs deeper.
Here’s the main thing that I want you to take away. Sometimes you don’t need a particular person at all. You just need a real human being, physically there with you, in the same place at the same time. And the fact that you know it’s a human is part of what you’re getting. That’s why this preference can hold up even when the machines are excellent. So let’s start somewhere hard, at the end of a life.
Imagine a small room in a hospice, at three in the morning. The person in the bed is in their late eighties, and they don’t have long. Now imagine that this room is in the future that this course is preparing for, where machines are better than humans at almost everything. A system at the nursing station watches every heartbeat and every breath. It adjusts the pain medication the moment the patient needs it. A robot turns the patient every couple of hours 
to prevent bedsores. And it does that more gently than any exhausted human on the night shift. It never runs late, and it never forgets a dose. And it calls the family the minute anything changes.
Every one of those things is genuinely good. Anyone in that bed would be glad of all of it. But there’s still something missing from that room. Nobody is holding their hand.
And this is not a rare scene. In 2022, about half of all the people on Medicare who died were enrolled in hospice at the time of death. That’s 
nearly fifty percent
. So hospice is not some rare corner of life. For a huge number of families, it’s where the story ends.
When I first laid out this framework, I put it this way. Sure, you might have a robot helping out at the nursing station. But when you’re near the end of life, you want a real human holding your hand. That person doesn’t have to do anything clever. They don’t have to say anything wise. They just have to be there. And there is some irreducible value in a human being who is physically present in that moment.
Now, notice that there are really two kinds of work in that room. Most of what a hospice does today is skilled labor, such as the medication, the monitoring, the lifting and the paperwork. A lot of that work is safe today only because the machines aren’t good enough yet. That’s 
the
 temporary kind of safety. But holding your hand is different. People don’t want a human holding their hand because the machines can’t do it yet. They want a human because it’s a human. And that is essential demand.
So let’s pin down exactly what presence is. Presence means that a real person is physically there with you, in the same place, at the same time. In one line, it’s “I want a real person here with me.”
And this is what makes presence different from affinity. Back in Lesson 5, affinity was “I want this 
particular 
person.” That’s your mom, your best friend, or the barber who knows exactly how you like your hair. Presence doesn’t need any of that. The person holding your hand in hospice might be a nurse or a volunteer that you met this week. You don’t know their middle name. You’ve never seen their house. And it still matters enormously that they’re there.
The same thing shows up in much happier places. Think about the music at a wedding. Any skilled musician will do, as long as a real person is playing. You don’t need Taylor Swift. Most couples couldn’t tell you the guitarist’s name without checking the invoice. But now imagine a robot string quartet. It’s flawless. It never misses a note, it never shows up late, and it never asks for a plate at the buffet. And for a lot of couples, something about that moment would still feel thinner. They weren’t asking for a particular person. They were asking for a
 real
 person.
So let’s run the course’s test on this. Imagine that a machine could deliver the same result, just as good and just as reliable, and that everyone knew it was a machine. Would people still want the human? For the playlist at the reception, plenty of people would happily take the machine. For the hand in the hospice, a lot of people would not. That gap is presence.
Now, “physically there” is doing real work in that definition. A video call is something. When a grandparent watches a birthday party on a phone, that’s a real person, live, at the same time. But it’s something less than presence. That 
grandparent can’t pick up the baby. They can’t hand you a tissue, and they can’t give you a hug on the way out the door. And everyone involved knows it, which is why people still fly across the country for birthdays, weddings and funerals. So presence has two parts. It’s a real human, and it’s that human in the room with you. Take away either part, and you get something less.
Let’s try a quick exercise that you can do in your head. Think of three moments in your life when you’d want a real person in the room, even a stranger. Maybe it’s a birth, a hospital bed, or the first time you tried something that scared you. 
If I was climbing up a mountain, sure I would want a robot to help carry the load and handle logistics. But I also want a real human who has a fear of death there to help me with my acrophobia. 
Now, for each one, imagine a perfect machine there instead, and you know that’s what it is. Is that enough? Wherever the answer is no, you’ve found presence.
Now let’s go from the end of a life to the very beginning of one.
Imagine a toddler at daycare. Back in Lesson 3, we pulled daycare apart into a bundle of different things. Some of it is derived demand. Somebody has to wash the bottles, wipe the tables, change the diapers and keep track of naps. Someday, a machine could do a lot of that work, and maybe more reliably than a tired person at the end of a long shift.
But then there’s the other part. Infants and toddlers are learning what a human being is. They watch faces. They learn what a frown means, and what a smile means, and who comes back when they cry. And this is where presence comes in. For childcare, you want someone who has actually had the 
experience of being a child, and of being a parent, to care for infants and toddlers. A robot has never been two years old. It has never been scared of the dark. And it has never been up at three in the morning with a crying baby of its own. That lived experience is part of what a human caregiver brings into the room. And parents feel this too. When you drop your kid off in the morning, you want to hand them to a person, someone who will look them in the eye.
Elder care works the same way, from the other direction. Think about helping an older person get out of bed and into a chair. If a machine can do that more safely and more comfortably, and the person stays in control of when and how, then plenty of people will prefer that. That’s not a rejection of human care. It’s just a better way to get into a chair. But that same person might still want a human to sit with them in the afternoon, to talk with them, and to notice them. So presence doesn’t mean that every task in care stays human. It means that some part of care stays human, because people want a person there.
And that’s the shape of a whole family of careers, which we’ll get to later in this course. In Lesson 10, we’ll look at people who accompany others through vulnerable experiences, such as doulas and end-of-life companions. In Lesson 11, it’s bodywork and personal instruction, where the human has to be in the room by definition. And in Lesson 13, it’s the adults who help children grow up among humans. For now, just notice what all of them have in common. The customer wants a person in the room, and that person has to be human.
Now, you might push back on all of this. Dave, if the robot is good enough, how would you even know? Remember the robots from Westworld, which we 
talked about in Lesson 1. Imagine one of them sitting at that hospice bedside. It looks human. It’s warm. Its hand feels exactly like a hand. So doesn’t presence just disappear?
No, and here’s why. Even if you have a robot that is indistinguishable from humans, many people will prefer the real human. That’s because the knowing is part of the experience. And we actually have some good evidence on this.
In 2025, a team of psychologists published nine experiments on exactly this question. People described something hard that they were going through, and they got back a supportive reply. Every single one of those replies was written by AI. But some of them were labeled as written by a human, and others as written by AI. And the replies that people believed came from a human were rated as more empathic and more supportive. Some people were even willing to wait days or weeks for a reply from a human.
Then another team came at it from the other side. In a study published in 2026, people got to choose whether they received empathy from a human or from an AI. They chose the human about 60 percent of the time. And here’s the funny part. When people actually read the AI responses, they rated them higher. They said that the machine was better at making them feel heard. And they still picked the human.
So the same words do a different job, depending on who you believe is behind them. A machine can copy the outward behavior perfectly. But that still isn’t the thing that people are asking for.
And I’ll tell you where I stand personally. For me, there’s a permanent uncanny valley. You know the uncanny valley. It’s that creepy feeling you get 
when a robot is almost human, but not quite. Most people treat it as a temporary problem. Just make the skin a little better and the eyes a little more natural, and eventually you climb out the other side. But I don’t think that I would ever climb out. An AI could simulate every emotion perfectly, and part of me would still get the signal that it’s not human, and that it’s only pretending. The valley isn’t really in the face. It’s in the knowing.
Now, as a quick aside, there are absolutely people who won’t care. There are many people who would actually prefer the lifelike robot, expressly because it isn’t human. There’s a line from Terminator 2, where Sarah Connor is watching the Terminator play with her son. She notes that 
the machine
 will never get tired, angry, or drunk. He’ll never hit him and never leave him. These are good reasons for some people to prefer a machine. Even so, many people will still prefer the genuine article of real human touch. And anyways, if we get to the point where machines really are that good, where they are warm, they breathe, and they bleed, maybe we won’t be worried about money at that point! 
And part of that knowing is about choice. When someone who could leave chooses to stay, that choice tells me something. The hospice volunteer could be at home asleep. The nurse could have gone home at the end of the shift. When a person stays anyway, it means something, precisely because they didn’t have to.
Now, I want to be fair. Not everyone has my metaphysical hang-ups. Some people will be perfectly happy with a robot companion, and I’m not going to judge anyone for wanting to be held. But essential demand doesn’t need everyone. It only needs enough people who want the real human, and who 
know that they’re getting one. That’s also why presence depends on honesty. If someone pays for a real person and quietly gets a machine, they didn’t get a cheaper version of the same thing. They got something else entirely.
To me, presence is the essential biological reality of humans. We are social animals. And at the start of life and at the end of it, when we’re at our most vulnerable, we want another human being right there with us.
 Our nervous systems are finely calibrated to want attunement—the millions of nonverbal cues that a real human is there with you, sharing the experience. It is the sublime experience of one consciousness recognizing another consciousness.
Okay, so here are the three big things from this lesson. First, presence means a real human being who is physically there with you, in the same place at the same time. A video call is something, but it’s something less. Second, presence is not affinity. You don’t need a particular person. The hand you’re holding might belong to someone you met this week, and any skilled musician will do for your wedding, as long as a real person is playing. And third, knowing that it’s a human is part of the experience. That’s why this preference holds up even when a machine could copy every outward behavior.
And that fits into the larger argument of this course. When machines can supply every result that we pay for, demand for humans persists for four reasons, and we’ve now covered three of them. Affinity says, “I want this person.” Accountability says, “Someone has to be on the hook.” And presence says, “I want a real person here with me.”
In Lesson 8, we’ll cover the last component, which is provenance. That’s when the fact that a human really made something is part of what you’re buying. See you there!
