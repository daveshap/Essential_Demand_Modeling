Welcome to Lesson 6. This one is about accountability, which is the second of the four components of essential demand. As a quick reminder, essential demand covers the cases where people want a human because of the human, not only because of the result. And here’s the big idea for this lesson. Accountability is not about who does the work. It’s about who answers for the work. People want someone on the hook, which means a real person with real power over a decision, and something real to lose if that decision goes wrong. And that can survive even when machines do every single task underneath the decision.
So let’s start with a thought experiment about the police.
Picture a small town, some years from now. Late on a Friday night, a patrol car pulls over a driver on the edge of town. But there’s no officer in the car. The whole police department has been replaced by machines. The machine that walks up to the driver’s window never loses its temper. It never acts on a grudge. It didn’t have a fight at home that morning, and it doesn’t care what the driver looks like.
Now, I want to be fair about why people imagine this. Human officials can be badly flawed. Some of them abuse their power. Some of them are biased. And some of them just aren’t very good at the job. That’s exactly why people imagine machines doing it better. So let’s grant the premise completely. Suppose the machine police make fewer mistakes than human police, on every measure that you care to name. There are fewer wrongful arrests and faster response times, and nobody gets hurt who didn’t have to.
So let’s run the test from this course. Imagine that a machine could deliver the same result, just as good and just as reliable, and that everyone knew it was a machine. Would people still want the human?
For policing, I think the answer is yes. And it’s not because the machine is worse. Think about the night that something does go wrong, because eventually something always does. Say the machine detains your teenager by mistake and holds them for six hours. You march down to city hall, and you ask who’s responsible. The clerk at the front desk is a machine too, and it’s very polite. And the answer comes back that the system performed within acceptable parameters. There’s nobody to fire. There’s nobody to vote out. There’s nobody to hold responsible who will ever feel a thing. That’s the future that I don’t want. You don’t want a faceless machine state that can crack down with no remorse. Even if the machines are 
hypothetically 
better, you will still want someone on the hook.
 There are also concerns about “who owns the machines?” Right now, cities and towns all across America are banning and removing Flock cameras, which are AI enabled surveillance devices. Why? Because the automated surveillance has been badly abused, and trust is 
sinking fast. If we don’t even want AI surveillance cameras, we probably don’t want privately owned Terminator police, either. 
So what does being on the hook actually look like? In America, we already have a good model for that, which is the county sheriff. As of 2026, about 98 percent of the sheriffs in the United States are elected by the people of their county. That means the sheriff has a boss, and the boss is the voters. If the sheriff runs the department badly, then the people can vote that sheriff out at the next election.
The chief of police works a little differently, but the idea doesn’t change. A chief is usually appointed by a mayor or a city manager, which means a chief can be fired. In many states, voters also get a say over their judges, either by electing them or by voting on whether to keep them. And an official who breaks the law can be charged, and can even go to jail.
 In some cases, public servants are also vulnerable to civil suits for wrongdoing. These are all incentives for good behavior. Remember that. 
So you want a sheriff that you can fire. You want a chief of police that you can fire. And you want people who can be sued or jailed. That matters for a simple reason. Accountability is fundamentally about incentives. The threat of consequences pushes people towards good behavior. It’s the same reason that a restaurant keeps its kitchen clean, because the health inspector could walk in on any given day. Nobody has to catch the cook doing something wrong. The chance of getting caught does most of the work.
A machine doesn’t feel that pressure. You can unplug a machine, but it won’t care. It has no career to lose, no reputation around town, and no family that 
has to look the neighbors in the eye. A human official has all of that at stake, and that’s the whole point.
Now, you might be thinking, why not just hold the city accountable? If the machine police make a mistake, then you sue the city, and the city’s insurance pays you. That’s a fair question, and money really can fix some kinds of damage. A city or an insurer can pay a claim without any human ever having done the work. But a check in the mail is not the same thing as someone on the hook. The check pays for the last mistake. What you also want is a person who has to stand in front of you and explain the next decision. People want the decisions that shape their lives to be made, or at least signed off
 on
, by someone who can hear their reasons, take the criticism, and lose the next election. You can stand up at a town meeting and yell at the sheriff. Try yelling at a server rack.
Now, in the last lesson, we talked about affinity, which is when you want a particular person. And there’s a neat way to see how accountability is different. Affinity means I don’t want this person replaced. Accountability means I need the power to replace this person. Those sound like opposites, and in a way they are. But both of them keep a human in the picture, just for completely different reasons.
In business, there’s a blunt phrase for this, which is the throat to choke. When a company buys a big, complicated system, the buyers want one throat to choke. They want one party that answers for the whole thing, so that when something breaks, nobody has to spend a month figuring out whose fault it was. 
That’s just how business works. You want someone with skin in the game.
It sounds a little violent. But every one of us already thinks this way.
For instance, take a bridge. When a licensed engineer in America puts their seal on a set of drawings, that seal means the engineer takes professional responsibility for what’s on those pages. If the bridge has a problem, the stamp says who answers for the design. A surgeon works the same way. The surgeon’s name is on your operation, and that surgeon answers for what happens in that room. A judge signs the 
warrant 
order. And the local business owner stands behind the work. When I had a fence put in, the contractor put his license, his name, and his reputation on the line. That’s a story for Lesson 9, but you can already see the pattern. A machine can recommend a verdict, a diagnosis, or a design. But someone has to sign it, own it, and face the consequences.
And this next part surprises people. Accountability can survive even when every task underneath it is automated. Imagine that, years from now, a machine designs the whole bridge and checks every calculation, and robots pour every yard of concrete. You still want a human engineer whose name is on that bridge. We saw the same thing in Lesson 4, with robots running into the burning building while the fire chief stays human. The work moves to the machines. The responsibility doesn’t have to.
And notice that this is not the temporary kind of safety, where a job is safe only because machines aren’t good enough yet. In this thought experiment, the machines are already better at the work. People keep the human on purpose, because they want someone to answer for that work.
 So, our bridge architect likely used AI to stress test his or her design. They used generative AI agents to simulate the bridge in a category 5 hurricane. That’s all fine, 
because the machines made the design better. But if the bridge kills people, our society demands accountability. 
But this is also where accountability gets counterfeited. And I want you to be able to spot the counterfeit.
Think about the front of your car. Modern cars have something called a crumple zone. The front end is designed to crush in a crash, so that the force goes into the metal instead of into you. The car sacrifices part of itself to protect the driver.
In 2019, a researcher who studies technology and society published a paper that turned that idea around. The paper called it the moral crumple zone. A moral crumple zone is a human who absorbs the blame when an automated system fails, even though that human had very little control over what the system did. A 
mechanical
 crumple zone protects the 
human 
driver. A moral crumple zone protects the machine
 (and the company that owns the machine)
, and the nearest human takes the hit.
 In other words, a “moral crumple zone” is a legal scapegoat to blame a human for a machine failure.
So imagine what that might look like at work. Say a company uses AI to approve or deny insurance claims. And say a rule requires a human to sign off on every decision. So the company hires one person, and that person approves 2,000 machine decisions a day. In an eight-hour day, that’s about fourteen seconds a decision, with no bathroom breaks. That person can’t really check anything, can’t change how the system works, and can’t hit the brakes. But their name is on every single claim. So when the machine denies a claim that it shouldn’t have, guess who gets fired. The person gets fired, and the system 
keeps right on running. That is not accountability. That’s a scapegoat with a login.
Engineers already have a name for the counterfeit version. It’s called plan-stamping, which means putting your seal on drawings that you didn’t actually direct. And the national society for professional engineers calls that unethical. A seal is supposed to mean that I directed this work, and I answer for it. It is not supposed to be a rubber stamp.
So here’s how to tell the real thing from the counterfeit. Meaningful accountability has two parts. The first part is real authority. The person can look inside the work, ask questions, change the method, fix the problem, and stop the whole thing if they need to.
 A good example of this is a commercial pilot’s license. In aviation law, the pilot has final authority over whether a plane flies. If the licensed pilot says the plane is unworthy to fly, it does not leave the ground. That is real authority with real veto power behind it. 
 The second part is real consequences. If it goes wrong, then that person loses something they care about, like their job, their license, their reputation, or even their freedom. 
That is what we mean by real stakes, incentive gradients, or “skin in the game.” 
If you have both parts, you have accountability. If all you have is a name on a form, then you have a moral crumple zone.
Some lawmakers are already writing the first part down. The European Union’s law on AI, which was passed in 2024, says that high-risk AI systems have to be built so that a human can oversee them. That human has to be able to override the system, and even halt it with something like a stop button. A stop button is real authority. A signature line, on its own, is not.
Here’s a quick exercise. Think about the last time that a service really let you down. Maybe it was a bank, an airline, or a repair shop. Now ask yourself two questions. Was there a real person who had the power to fix the problem? And did that person have anything to lose if they didn’t? If the answer to both questions is no, then you’ve already met the faceless machine state, just in miniature.
 (To be fair, most state administrators are like this. If you’ve ever had to go to the county office to ask about a bill, there was nothing the clerk could do, and no recourse either… but I digress.)
So what does all of this mean for making a living? Accountability is not a job of doing tasks. It’s a job of owning outcomes. Think about the home inspector who signs the report before you buy a house. The inspector’s tools might do most of the measuring, but the inspector’s name is on that report. Or think about the captain of an airliner. The autopilot does most of the flying on a long flight, but the captain is still in command, and the captain answers for that flight. In both cases, what you’re really paying for is a person who is willing to say that this one is on them.
And I want to be honest about one thing. In my own research on essential demand, I make the point that there are two different questions here. The first question is whether a human has to remain at all. The second question is how many hours of human work that actually takes. One engineer’s seal might someday cover the work that used to take a whole team of drafters. So accountability keeps the human role, but it doesn’t keep every job that used to sit underneath that role. The people who earn a living from accountability are the ones willing to put their name and their neck on the line. And that’s exactly where the career pathways begin.
Okay, so here are the three things that we covered. First, accountability means that someone is on the hook. The threat of real consequences is what pushes people towards good behavior, which is why you want a sheriff that you can fire. Second, meaningful accountability takes real authority plus real consequences. A human name stamped on a machine’s decision is not accountability. It’s a moral crumple zone. And third, accountability can survive even when every task underneath it is automated. Affinity means I don’t want this person replaced, but accountability means I need the power to replace this person. In the larger argument of this course, derived demand goes to the machines, and essential demand is what stays human. Accountability is the component that most often survives right in the middle of a heavily automated service. And it’s the foundation for the first career pathway, the accountable local business owner, in Lesson 9.
