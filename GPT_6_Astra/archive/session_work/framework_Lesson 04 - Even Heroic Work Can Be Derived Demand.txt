Even Heroic Work Can Be Derived Demand
Lesson 4 of 17: firefighters, soldiers, and the outcome we actually want
Pronunciation and sources (not read aloud):  
No names need a note · Sources: CBS Los Angeles, LAFD’s new firefighting robot rolls into action (2020); Los Angeles Times, Baptism by fire for L.A.’s flame terminator (2020); Route Fifty, Firefighting robot joins LA force (2020); US Air Force, MQ-9 Reaper fact sheet; US Navy, Mk 15 Phalanx CIWS fact file; Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, Autonomy in Weapon Systems (2023); Congressional Research Service, IF11150 (2024); David Shapiro, Post-Labor Economics, Derived vs Essential Demand v01 (2026)
Welcome back. This is Lesson 4, and it takes on the hardest case for derived demand, which is heroic work. We’re talking about firefighters, and soldiers, and everyone else who runs towards danger so that the rest of us don’t have to. And the main thing that I want you to take away is this. Admiring the people who do dangerous work is not the same thing as wanting people in danger. Even the bravest jobs are bought for an outcome, and that outcome is what we actually want. Along the way, I’m also going to give you a tool that works on any job, including yours. You split the job into three parts, which are the execution, the relationships, and the responsibilities.
So let’s start with a fire.
It’s two o’clock in the morning, in an ordinary house on an ordinary street. A family is asleep upstairs, and let’s say that it’s yours. There are two parents, a toddler, a baby, a dog, and a cat. Then the alarm goes off, and by the time anyone is awake, the stairs are full of smoke and the hallway is burning.
The fire trucks arrive within minutes, and the firefighters do what firefighters do. They suit up, they pull the hoses, and they head for the house. These are brave people. But they are also human beings, with lungs and skin and families of their own. A human firefighter can only stand so much heat, and can only breathe the air in the tank on their back. And when a burning building starts to look like it might come down, the person in charge sometimes has to pull the crews out. That isn’t cowardice. A firefighter who dies in a collapse doesn’t save anyone.
Now I want you to imagine a different truck pulling up. Out of the back walks a robot. This robot can withstand 500 degrees. It doesn’t breathe, so the smoke doesn’t matter. And this is the key part. It doesn’t have to protect its own life first. So it walks straight up the burning stairs, and it carries out you, and your babies, and your cats and dogs.
So let’s run the test from this course. Imagine that a machine could deliver the same result, just as good and just as reliable, and that everyone knew it was a machine. Would people still want the human? Picture those parents standing on the lawn in their pajamas, watching that robot go in. Not one of them is going to say, hold on, I’d rather wait for a human. In this case, the machine doesn’t just match the human. It beats the human, because it can go where no person can survive. I would argue that’s actually superior to a human firefighter, even if the fire chief is human.
So what did that family actually want? They wanted to live. They wanted the kids out, and the pets out, and maybe the house still standing. The firefighters were how they got that. And that is derived demand, which means that the 
demand for someone’s labor comes from the demand for the result that their labor produces.
Now, I can already hear the objection, and it’s a fair one. Isn’t this a little disrespectful? Firefighters are heroes. They put their lives on the line for total strangers. Are we really going to say that their work is just a means to an end?
Yes, we are, and I think it’s the most respectful thing that we can say. Think about why we admire firefighters in the first place. We admire them because of the risk. They walk into the thing that everyone else is running away from. But that risk is exactly the part that nobody wants. No family wants a firefighter to die saving them. No town wants to hold that funeral. So the very thing that makes the job heroic is the thing that we would remove if we could. You can honor someone’s sacrifice while wanting a future in which nobody has to make it.
And this isn’t only a thought experiment. In October 2020, the Los Angeles Fire Department got a remote-controlled firefighting robot. It looks like a small tank, on treads, with a water cannon on top. It was supposed to make its debut at a press conference. But that morning, a big fire broke out downtown, so the department took the robot straight there instead. A firefighter drives the robot with a controller, and the operator can stand up to about five football fields away from the danger.
The fire chief at the time explained why the department wanted it. He said that large fires sometimes force the department to pull its firefighters back, because of the risk that the building will collapse. And then he said this, and I quote. “I can afford to lose one of these wonderful machines. I cannot afford to lose a firefighter.”
Notice who said that. It was a human fire chief, choosing to send a machine where he didn’t want to send a person. That robot doesn’t make the department any less human. It makes the department better at the thing that it’s actually for.
Now let’s take the same question to national defense, which is the biggest example of all. When your tax dollars pay for the military, what are you actually paying for? You don’t care about any individual soldier, or pilot, or general. Yes, of course, you might admire the armed services in principle. But the outcome that you really want is not to be attacked by a foreign adversary. You don’t want a missile to hit your home. You don’t want bioterrorism. Protection is the product. The people are just how we get that protection today.
And defense is already heavily automated. For instance, take the Reaper drone. It’s an armed aircraft that the Air Force flies with nobody on board. Its crew is a pilot and a sensor operator, who sit at a ground control station. And that station can be back in the United States, while the aircraft flies its mission overseas. The mission goes on, but the pilot no longer has to sit in the aircraft. Or look at the Navy. Many of its ships carry a radar-guided gun that can detect, track, and fire on an incoming missile automatically. Add in cyber defenses, radar networks, and every other kind of drone, and a lot of the watching and the doing is already done by machines.
So why do we still send young people into harm’s way? Here’s my answer. We have to have humans because machines aren’t good enough yet. That’s the temporary kind of safety from Lesson 1. It lasts exactly as long as the machines are worse than people at the job, and not a day longer. Taking 
human soldiers out of harm’s way might be the better option, so that we don’t have to bury young men and women who get caught in the line of fire.
But notice what doesn’t go away. In 2023, the Defense Department updated its official rules on autonomy in weapons. Those rules say that these systems have to be designed so that commanders and operators can exercise, and I quote, “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force.” In other words, the machines can do more and more of the flying, the watching, and even the shooting. But a human still has to own the decision. That’s the fire chief again, just in a uniform.
So let’s get to the tool that I promised you, and it works on any job. Instead of asking whether a whole job is safe, split it into three parts. The first part is the execution, which is the actual doing of the work. The second part is the relationships, which means the people who want you in particular, or want a real person there with them. And the third part is the responsibilities, which means who makes the call, and who answers for the decision when things go wrong.
Let’s run firefighting through all three. The execution is the hose, the ladder, the axe, and the carrying. That’s the part a 500-degree robot could do better, and it’s most of the hours on the job. The relationships are thinner than you might think. Most people couldn’t name a single firefighter at their local station. When the trucks show up, you want the fire out, and you don’t care whose face is behind the mask. And then come the responsibilities. Someone has to decide whether to send the robots up the stairs or pull them back. Someone has to answer to the city and to the public when a building burns 
down that should have been saved. That’s the fire chief, and the fire chief stays human.
Now, there’s a catch, and it matters. A human role is not the same thing as a whole occupation. The chief stays. But a department with one chief and a fleet of robots does not need the same number of people as a department full of firefighters. So keeping one human on the hook does not keep every job behind that person. That’s the difference between keeping a role and keeping a workforce.
So let’s do a quick exercise. Think about your own job, and split it in your head into those three parts. What do you actually do all day? Who wants you, specifically, and not just the result? And what are you on the hook for?
For instance, say that you run payroll for a small company. The execution is the numbers, the forms, and the deadlines. The relationships might be the coworkers who come to you with questions, because they trust you. And the responsibilities are signing off when the paychecks go out, and answering for the mistake when one of them is wrong. For most people, the execution is the biggest of the three parts by far. And the execution is exactly what the machines are coming for.
The fire chief is also your first glimpse of something this course will come back to, which is accountability. It’s one of the four components of essential demand, and it means that someone has to be on the hook. We’ll dig into accountability properly in Lesson 6. For now, just notice that a human can stay in charge of a service that is almost entirely automated.
There’s one more twist, and it might be the most important one. What does the public really want from a fire department? It isn’t a great firefight. It’s no fire at all. The best emergency is the one that never happens. That’s what smoke alarms are for, and sprinklers, and building codes, and the inspector who catches the bad wiring before it ever sparks. Every one of those means less firefighting, and nobody misses it.
Defense works the same way. The best war is the one that never starts, because the other side looked at your defenses and decided not to try. And the best missile attack is the one that gets stopped long before it reaches your street. So the outcome that people want is not more heroic work. The outcome is no disasters. And humans are only instrumental to that outcome. In fact, you could argue that the optimal state is no humans in harm’s way at all.
Now let’s step back and look at the road so far. In Lesson 2, we looked at the things you buy, like your house, your car, and your refrigerator. In Lesson 3, we looked at the invisible workforce behind your lights and your internet. And in this lesson, we looked at the most admired work there is. All three come out the same way. How much a mission matters is a separate question from whether it needs human labor. Protecting your family might be the most important job in the world. That still doesn’t mean that the job has to be done by human hands.
One last point about essential demand. The original test I came up for this, way back before I had even coined the term essential demand, was this: “in what situations will a human be willing to pay a premium just for human input?” That part of the original thought experiment is what led directly to the difference between derived demand (where someone will go for the cheapest 
option that is good enough) and essential demand (where people will pay extra just for the privilege of human involvement.) 
Okay, so here are the three big things from this lesson. First, admiring dangerous work is not the same as wanting people in danger. Firefighting and defense are bought for an outcome, which is staying alive and safe, so the work behind them is derived demand. Second, the best version of that work might put nobody in harm’s way. Better still, there’s no disaster in the first place. And third, you can split any job into its execution, its relationships, and its responsibilities. The execution is where the machines go first, and the responsibilities are where a human can stay on the hook.
And that’s how this lesson fits into the larger argument. It finishes the first part of this course, which is all about derived demand. From here, we turn to the other side, which is essential demand. That’s the work that people want from a human because it’s human. In Lesson 5, we’ll start with the first of its four components, which is affinity. That’s when people want one particular person, and nobody else will do. I’ll see you in the next lesson!
