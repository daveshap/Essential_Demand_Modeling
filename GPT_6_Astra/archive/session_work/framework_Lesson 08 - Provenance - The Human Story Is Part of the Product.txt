Welcome to Lesson 8, the last of the four components of essential demand. This one is called provenance. As a quick reminder, essential demand is when people want a human because of the human, not only because of the result. Provenance is the simplest version of that idea. A real human made it.
And here’s the one idea that I want you to carry out of this lesson. Sometimes what you’re paying for isn’t just the object. It’s the story of how that object came to exist. Two things can look and work exactly alike, and one of them is still worth more, because a person actually made it. But provenance comes with a catch. A human story can be recorded once and then copied forever. By the end of this lesson, you’ll know which kind of provenance keeps people working, and which kind doesn’t. So let’s start in the kitchen.
It’s a 
Wednesday
 night, and you’re standing at the counter, chopping an onion for dinner. The knife in your hand came from a department store. It was probably made in a factory on the other side of the world, and you have no idea whose hands were on it. You don’t care, either. You bought that knife for 
one reason, which is cutting. If it’s sharp, holds an edge, and doesn’t slip in your hand, then it did its job.
That’s derived demand. As a reminder from Lesson 2, derived demand means that the demand for someone’s labor comes from the demand for the result their labor produces. You wanted a sharp knife. The people at the factory were just how you got one. If a robot made the next knife, just as sharp and a little cheaper, you would buy it without a second thought.
Now, there’s a second knife in that drawer. Your friend made it. They spent a year of weekends learning to forge steel in their garage, and they burned their fingers more than once. They ground the blade, shaped the handle, and gave it to you for your birthday. Maybe it isn’t even as sharp as the store knife. Maybe it’s a little heavy. But if your house was on fire, which knife would you grab?
That’s provenance. Provenance means that a real human made it, and the human story behind a thing is part of what you value. In one line, it’s the customer saying, “It matters that a human really made this.” The effort, the skill, the struggle, and your relationship with the maker are all baked into that object. And I think that’s the heart of provenance. The human story behind something, the human suffering, and the human skill are worth something to us.
There’s a phrase going around social media for this, which is proof of effort. When a human actually puts in the time and energy, the thing they made is just more valuable. And notice that this has nothing to do with how well the knife cuts. For cutting, the factory knife might win. But for everything else that your friend’s knife means to you, there is no contest.
 The finger painting your 
child made at school is worth more to you than any generic decoration because it came from her little hands. That is provenance. 
So let’s run the test from this course. Imagine that a machine could deliver the same result, just as good and just as reliable, and that everyone knew it was a machine. Would people still want the human? For the store knife, no. For your friend’s knife, the question barely makes sense. A robot could forge an identical blade, down to the last scratch. But it would not be the knife your friend made for you. The steel would be identical, and the object would be completely different.
Now, you might be thinking that this is just sentiment between friends. So let’s take the friend out of it.
In 2023, a team of psychologists ran a simple experiment with paintings. They showed people thirty paintings, and every single one of them was made by AI. Then they randomly labeled some of those paintings as the work of a human, and the rest as the work of AI. People rated each painting on how much they liked it, how beautiful it was, how profound it was, and how much it was worth.
The paintings labeled human won across the board. And the gap was biggest on profundity and worth, which are the questions about meaning. So think about what happened there. Nobody touched the paintings. Not one pixel changed. The researchers changed one line of text, and people saw more depth in the exact same image. Changing the label changed the judgment, without changing the painting.
So is that just people being fooled? No. Those people weren’t judging pixels. They were judging what they believed had happened, which is that a person saw something, felt something, and tried to put it on a canvas. When the label said a machine did it, that story disappeared, and a lot of the value went with it. People care about what truly happened, even when they can’t see it in the finished product.
You can see this all over ordinary life. People pay more for a chair made by hand, by a woodworker who chose the board and cut every joint. You sit in it exactly the same way that you sit in a flat-pack chair. But only one of those two chairs comes with a story.
The memoir is the clearest case of all. A memoir is valuable because someone actually lived it. Now imagine that you found out the war memoir on your nightstand was written by a machine that never went anywhere. Every word on the page would be identical. But it wouldn’t be a memoir anymore. It would just be fiction with a person’s name on the cover.
Provenance gets even stronger when the object is a gift. In 2015, three marketing researchers published four studies on what they called the handmade effect. They showed people everyday products, such as ceramic mugs and soap, described as either handmade or made by machine. People found the handmade versions more attractive. And when the researchers dug into why, a big part of the answer wasn’t quality at all. It was that people believed handmade things symbolically contain love.
And this is the part that matters most for this lesson. The effect depended on who the gift was for. When people were buying for someone they loved, they clearly preferred handmade. And when the point of the gift was to show love, 
they would pay more for it. But when the gift was for someone more distant, that preference didn’t show up. So nobody needs a handmade mug for the office gift exchange with a coworker they’ve met twice. For your mom’s birthday, though, it’s a different story.
That’s provenance expressing care. A gift is a message, and the human making is part of that message. Think about the lopsided clay bowl that a kid brings home from art class. By any market measure, it’s worth almost nothing. But plenty of parents keep that bowl for decades, because the whole value is that their kid made it. When someone gives you something and says, “I made this with my own bare hands,” the object turns into a story.
And a machine can copy the object, but it cannot copy that message. Say your friend bought a robot-forged knife, told you they made it, and you found out later. Nothing about the knife would change. The gift would be ruined. So provenance only works if the story is true.
Let’s try a quick exercise. Look around the room you’re in right now, and find the thing that you’d be most upset to lose. Odds are, it isn’t the most expensive thing in the room. It might be a quilt that your grandmother stitched, a letter in someone’s handwriting, or a drawing that a kid made for you. Now ask yourself why. If a machine could make a flawless replica tomorrow, would that replica mean as much to you? For most of us, the answer is no, and the reason is provenance.
Now let’s take provenance out of the garage and into a global industry. Video games are a lot like the Sony television from Lesson 2. A big game takes hundreds of people to build, and most players couldn’t name a single one of them. You want the game to be fun, and that’s all you care about.
But there’s a famous exception. Hideo Kojima is a Japanese game designer, best known for the Metal Gear spy series. He’s a legendary game maker, and I’d argue that his name is what sells his work. And his games carry a credit right on the box. It says, “A Hideo Kojima Game.” That’s not a company or a brand. That’s one man’s name.
In 2015, Kojima and his longtime publisher were parting ways. Before his last Metal Gear came out, the publisher removed “A Hideo Kojima Game” from the box art. Fans noticed, and plenty of them were angry. Then Kojima set up his own studio, and in 2019, he released a new game called Death Stranding. The credit came right back. So people asked him what that credit means. Kojima said that it means he was personally involved in the concept, the original story, the script, and the game design, from start to finish.
So why would anyone fight over one line of text on a box? Because for a Kojima fan, that line is a promise about who really made the game. And that’s provenance and affinity working together. Affinity, from Lesson 5, is “I want this person.” Provenance is “It matters that a human really made this.” The fan wants Kojima in particular, and it matters to them that his hands were really on the game. So let’s run the test one more time. If a machine made a game in his style, just as good, and everyone knew it was a machine, would that still be “A Hideo Kojima Game”? No. It would only be an imitation.
Now, there’s a nuance here that I want you to hold onto, because it matters a lot for your career. Provenance can be captured once and then replayed forever.
Go back to 1927. Before then, movies were silent, and in theater after theater, live musicians played along with the film. There was a pianist, an organist, or 
sometimes a whole orchestra in the pit, at every showing, night after night, in towns all over the country. Then the movies got sound. Now the music could be recorded once, by one orchestra in one studio, and the same reel could play anywhere. By the count of the musicians’ union in Los Angeles, about 22,000 of those theater jobs disappeared within three years. Only a few hundred new jobs appeared, playing on soundtracks.
And this is the strange part. The audience never stopped hearing human music. The music on those soundtracks was still played by real musicians. The provenance survived, but the jobs didn’t. One performance served everyone.
That’s the difference between a stock of human contribution and a flow of human work. A song is recorded once and played a billion times. A tattoo has to be made fresh on every single arm. And it’s true of old art, too. A painting by a dead master can sell for a fortune, but that money cannot buy one more picture from the artist.
So the careers built on provenance are the ones where every piece needs new making. That means a commissioned portrait, a ring made for one couple, a tattoo, or the knife your friend forges for your birthday. Each one takes a new stretch of a real person’s time, and nobody can copy and paste that. We’ll dig into those careers in Lesson 12, on art, music, and creative practice.
And with that, we’ve now covered all four components of essential demand. So let’s put them side by side, one line each. Affinity is “I want this person,” like the fans who came to see the real Taylor Swift on stage, not a hologram. Accountability is “Someone has to be on the hook,” like the sheriff that you can fire. Presence is “I want a real person here with me,” like someone holding your hand at the end of life. And provenance is “It matters that a human really 
made this,” like your friend’s knife. There may be more, but I think these four cover the vast majority of cases. And most real work doesn’t run on just one of them. The careers that hold up best combine two, three, or even all four. And that’s exactly what the career pathways ahead are built on.
Okay, so here are the three big things from this lesson. First, provenance means that a real human made it, and the story, effort, and skill behind a thing are part of what you’re buying. That’s your friend’s knife. Second, provenance is real even when you can’t see it. A label can change the value of a painting, a handmade gift carries care, and one man’s name can sell a game. And third, provenance can be recorded once and replayed forever, so the work that lasts is the work that has to be made fresh, one piece at a time.
That finishes the foundation of this course. We started with derived demand, which is the work that machines can take over. Then we covered essential demand and its four components, which are the reasons people will still want a human. From here on, we turn those reasons into actual ways to make a living.
