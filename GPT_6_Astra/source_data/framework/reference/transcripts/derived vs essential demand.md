# Essential Demand

What David said on the finished recording "derived vs essential demand" (8:05), recorded 15 Oct 2026.

Some people scoff at the idea that all of the jobs are going away. On that point, we actually agree with them. Post-labor economics doesn't mean zero jobs forever. It means that human labor is no longer a binding constraint. So the real question is which work is left, and why.

The answer starts in 1890. That year, the British economist Alfred Marshall published his book Principles of Economics, and he used a house to explain an idea that he called derived demand. People who buy a house want the house. They don't care who built it, how many people it took, or what the builders' names were. The demand for the builders' labor is derived from the demand for the house. The builders are merely instrumental to the outcome, and the outcome is what the buyer actually wants.

Marshall saw that nearly every kind of labor works this way. That is true for the firms that hire workers, and it is also true for the vast majority of goods and services that people want to buy. When you pay for a clean shirt, you want the shirt, not the person who pressed it. When a package arrives, the driver's name does not matter. And when your taxes are filed, you want the return, not the accountant who prepared it.

Derived demand is exactly the kind of demand that a machine can meet. If a robot can build the same house, just as well and for less money, the buyer will take the house built by the robot.

Marshall named derived demand in 1890, and its complement went without a name for 136 years. I call that complement essential demand. Essential demand covers the cases where people want a human because of the human, not only because of the result. It is the essential human input, the part that a machine cannot replace.

I used to call this the Taylor Swift effect. A robot Taylor Swift or a hologram Taylor Swift would not satisfy her fans, because they want the genuine article.

Earlier ideas came close. The experience economy, the attention economy, and the meaning economy each describe markets where people pay for something human. But each of them describes only one part of the larger picture. The higher-order principle underneath all of them is essential demand.

All demand for human labor falls into one of these two kinds, derived or essential, and many jobs have a little bit of both. There is a simple test for telling them apart. Imagine that a machine could deliver the same result, just as good and just as reliable, and that everyone knew it was a machine. If people would still want the human, that is essential demand. If they wouldn't, that is derived demand.

A chef is a good example of one job that has both. When a chef cooks for a cafeteria, the diners want a hot meal, and that is derived demand. But when the same chef cooks a small dinner for guests who came to see that particular chef, that is essential demand.

So how much of today's work is essential? My research includes a rough audit of every detailed occupation that the Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks, which covers about 170 million American jobs. The central estimate is that only about 5 percent of that work rests on essential demand. Even the most generous scenario comes in under 15 percent. These numbers are judgment calls rather than measurements, because no government survey asks whether a customer truly wants a human. But every version of the estimate points the same direction. The vast majority of human work today is derived demand. This was also true in Alfred Marshall's day, back in 1890.

Essential demand comes in four types. The first is presence, the second is provenance, the third is accountability, and the fourth is affinity.

Presence means that you want an actual person there, but not necessarily a particular person. Any skilled musician will do for your wedding, as long as a real person is playing. A conversation, a ceremony, and a nurse at your bedside all work the same way.

Provenance is about how something came to exist. People pay more for a chair made by hand, a painting by a human artist, or a memoir from someone who actually lived it. The value is in the human origin. But a human performance can be recorded and then replayed. When movies got sound in 1927, around 22,000 theater musicians lost their jobs within three years. Audiences still wanted human music, but they heard it on a soundtrack instead.

Accountability means that a human has to answer for a decision. A machine can recommend a verdict, a diagnosis, or a strategy, but someone has to sign it, own it, and face the consequences. Judges, surgeons, and company directors all carry that kind of weight.

And affinity means you want this particular person. That is the Taylor Swift effect. It is also why Robert Downey Jr. has said that he intends to sue any future studio executive who recreates his Iron Man with AI. He even joked that after he's dead, his law firm will still be very active. But affinity isn't only about celebrities. It's about your family doctor, your kid's favorite teacher, and the barber who knows exactly how you like your hair.

So what is left after automation is essential demand. And essential demand is real, but it is thin. American households spend about 490 billion dollars a year on entertainment. Even if every one of those dollars went straight to workers, at 60,000 dollars a year each, it would only fund about 8 million jobs. There are about 170 million jobs in America today.

In 1983, a Nobel Prize-winning economist compared human workers to horses. On farms, tractors first reduced the role of horses, and then they completely eliminated it. That economist predicted that machines would do the same thing to human workers. In 1915, American farms had more than 21 million horses. Today, there are only about 6.6 million horses in the whole country, and many of them are kept for riding, sport, and companionship.

In 2015, two economists at MIT answered that question. They wrote that humans are not horses. People have wants that only other people can satisfy, and that is what I call essential demand. And people can own capital, while horses can't. That difference is the other half of post-labor economics.

So post-labor economics doesn't mean zero jobs. It means that derived demand goes to the machines, and the work that stays human is the work where people actually want a human. That is essential demand. And for everyone else, the question becomes who owns the machines.
