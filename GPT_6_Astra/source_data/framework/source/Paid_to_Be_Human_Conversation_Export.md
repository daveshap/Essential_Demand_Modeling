The research offers several useful anchors.

Provenance has particularly clean experimental support. Bellaiche and colleagues (2023) assigned “human-created” or “AI-created” labels to paintings that were actually all AI-generated. There is also evidence involving money. This was a Mother’s Day gift context, and the broader study found that the effect depended on the relationship and purpose of the gift. That specificity is useful for the course: human provenance matters especially where the purchase expresses care or meaning.

Human engagement can remain desirable even when AI performs well. Rubin and colleagues (2025) conducted nine studies involving 6,282 participants. AI-generated supportive responses were evaluated more positively when attributed to humans. The words alone did not determine the experience; believing that a person was responding mattered. These experiments concern perceived human engagement, rather than physical co-presence specifically.

A particularly relevant 2026 paper found that participants preferred to receive human empathy even while rating the AI responses they received more highly. Across four studies, human responses were chosen approximately 57–62% of the time. That demonstrates coexistence: substantial demand for automated support alongside a preference for humans among other users. It does not establish how much extra customers would pay.

Affinity extends well beyond celebrity. A study of 562 hairstylist clients in Johannesburg linked commercial friendship with loyalty to the individual stylist, and behavioral loyalty with willingness to pay a premium. This was observational research, so it cannot isolate the relationship from every other service advantage. Nevertheless, it gives us a grounded example of affinity operating at an ordinary local-business scale.

For the course, that suggests an accessible pathway to investigate: becoming a particular person whom a manageable number of customers repeatedly choose. Fame is only one way to develop affinity.

Accountability includes the legitimacy of human authority. I’d start with the student as a customer. Have them recognize their own buying decisions, then give that intuition a name. That makes derived demand concrete before we connect it to their job.

The central teaching question is: What are you actually paying for?

Marshall does use housebuilding, specifically explaining how demand for houses creates demand for plasterers and the other building trades. That gives us the historical anchor, without needing an economics lecture.

With the house, I’d walk through three steps:

- You want a house: the right location, enough space, sound construction, a price you can afford.
- Delivering that house requires work: foundations, framing, wiring, plumbing, roofing.
- That creates demand for workers: they are hired because their work delivers the house you want.

Then introduce the definition: Derived demand means the demand for someone’s labor comes from demand for the result their labor produces. That is also how introductory economics texts explain the term.

Only then introduce automation: imagine the same house, with the same quality and safety, could be delivered using automated construction. Would the absence of human construction workers make the house less desirable to you?

That gets students to the consequential insight themselves: demand for the house can remain even if producing it requires less human labor.

I’d reinforce this with a short run of familiar examples. These are illustrative buying situations, so we can describe what the customer wants without claiming every customer thinks identically.

| --- | --- | --- |
