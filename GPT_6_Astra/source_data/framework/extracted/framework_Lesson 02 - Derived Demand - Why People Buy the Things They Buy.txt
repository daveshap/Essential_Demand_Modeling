Welcome to Lesson 2. In this one, we’re going to look at the most common kind of demand in the whole economy, which is derived demand. We’ll start with the things that you buy, from your house to your television. Then we’ll look at the services that you pay for, and at a job that has already disappeared. And if you remember just one thing from this lesson, make it this. When you buy almost anything, you’re paying for a result. You’re not paying for the particular people who produced it. Those people matter, and their work is real. But to you, the customer, they’re a means to an end. And that is exactly the kind of demand that a machine can meet.
So with that said, let’s start with the biggest thing that most people ever buy.
Picture a young couple on a Saturday afternoon, walking through a starter home with a real estate agent. They open the kitchen cabinets. They run the shower to check the water pressure. One of them looks up at the ceiling for water stains, while the other one quietly does the math on the mortgage. They have one problem to solve. Is this house good enough, and can they afford it?
Now notice what they don’t ask. They don’t ask who poured the foundation. They don’t ask who framed the walls, or who ran the wiring, or what the roofer’s name was. That’s not because they’re rude. It’s because none of that is what they’re buying. So here’s the question that runs through this whole lesson. What are you actually paying for?
With a house, the answer comes in three steps. The first step is that you want a house. You want the right location, enough space, a roof that doesn’t leak, and a price that you can afford. The second step is that delivering that house takes work. Somebody has to dig the foundation, frame the walls, and put in the plumbing, the wiring, and the roof. And the third step is that all of that work creates demand for workers. The carpenters, the electricians, and the roofers get hired because their work delivers the house that you want.
None of this is new. In 1890, the British economist Alfred Marshall published a book called Principles of Economics. And in that book, he used a house to explain this very idea. Marshall wrote that the demand for houses gives rise to a demand for the labor of all the various building trades. He even singled out the plasterers. Nobody buys a house because they want to hire a plasterer. People want the house, and the demand for the plasterer’s labor is derived from the demand for the house. So Marshall called it derived demand.
Let me give you the definition, because it’s worth holding on to. Derived demand means that the demand for someone’s labor comes from the demand for the result that their labor produces. The workers are instrumental to the outcome. 
But

, not the human labor.
Now let’s bring in the machines. Imagine that our couple could buy the very same house, in the same neighborhood, just as good and just as safe, but built entirely by machines. Would they want it any less? I don’t think so. Most people just want a house that is good enough, at a price that’s as low as possible. You probably don’t know the names of the people who built your home. You might not even know the name of the contractor. That house has almost zero human provenance, which is the human story behind a thing. We’ll come back to provenance in Lesson 8.
And here’s the test that we’ll use for the whole course. Imagine that a machine could deliver the same result, just as good and just as reliable, and that everyone knew it was a machine. Would people still want the human? For the house, the honest answer is no. And that tells you something uncomfortable about construction jobs. People still build houses today because machines aren’t good enough yet. That’s a real kind of safety, but it’s only temporary.
 Consider that robotic labor will ultimately make houses better and cheaper. The human labor loses on market competition. 
The house is the biggest example, but it isn’t unusual. Look around your own home, and you’ll find derived demand almost everywhere.
Take your car. Let’s say that you drive a Honda Civic. What you want from that Civic is pretty simple. You want it to start on a cold morning, get you to work, keep you safe if somebody runs a red light, and not cost a fortune at the pump. Unless you’re buying a luxury car, you probably don’t know who designed it. You’ve never met the engineers who worked on the brakes, or the people on the assembly line who bolted in the seats. And you don’t care. That’s not an insult to them. You just want the car.
Or take your refrigerator. Think about the last time you opened it at midnight, looking for a snack. You wanted the milk to be cold. You wanted the leftovers to still be good. And you wanted the whole thing to fit in the gap between your cabinets without running up the electric bill. Somewhere, people designed that refrigerator, built it, and loaded it onto a truck. You probably don’t know a single one of their names. And that doesn’t make the milk one degree warmer.
Now take your television. Let’s say that you’re a big fan of Sony. You’ve owned three Sony TVs, and you wouldn’t buy anything else. That sounds like loyalty, and in a way, it is. But who are you actually loyal to? It’s not the engineer who tuned the picture, because you have no idea who that is. You’re loyal to a company that has given you a great picture, again and again. Sony could change its factories, its suppliers, and half of its engineers, and as long as the picture stayed just as good, you’d keep buying. That’s brand recognition. A brand is trust in a company. It is not attachment to a person.
Now, you might push back here. You might say, wait a minute, I really care about my car, and I spent weeks picking out that TV. So does derived demand just mean that people buy the cheapest thing? No. Derived never means cheap, generic, or unimportant. Someone can care deeply about the way a car handles on a curve. Someone can fall in love with the architecture of an old house. And someone can obsess over the picture on a television. But notice that all of those are qualities of the result. The handling is part of the car, and the picture is part of the TV. You can be incredibly picky about what you buy, and the demand for the people who made it is still derived.
But one case works differently, and I want to set it aside for later. Hideo Kojima is a legendary video game designer. In 2019, his studio released a game called Death Stranding, and it was published by Sony, the same company that makes your television. But that game carries a different name. The credit reads, “A Hideo Kojima Game.” Kojima has explained that the credit means he’s involved in making the game from start to finish. And I think that’s exactly why he sells games, because his name is attached to them. His fans aren’t trusting a company. They want this particular person’s work. That’s our first glimpse of something different, which is essential demand. We’ll come back to Kojima in Lesson 8. For now, just notice the contrast. Sony is a brand. Kojima is a person.
So far, we’ve talked about things. But derived demand isn’t only about products. It also covers a lot of the services that you pay for, including some that feel very personal.
Take the plumber. Picture a Sunday night, with water spreading across your kitchen floor, and a plumber lying on their back under your sink with a flashlight. This is a skilled person. They’re inside your home, in your most private space, and the bill isn’t going to be small. That all feels personal. But what are you actually paying for? You want the leak to stop, and you want the water to flow the way that it should. If a machine could fix that leak just as well and just as safely, most of us would let the machine in.
Or take a cracked phone screen. You drop your phone in a parking lot, and the glass splinters out from one corner. So you take it to a repair shop, and you hand it to someone behind the counter. Did you catch their name? Maybe. Did 
it matter? Not really. You wanted your phone to work again, and you wanted it back by tomorrow.
So a service can be skilled, it can be expensive, and it can even happen inside your own home. And it can still be bought mainly for its result. Keep that in mind, because in the next lesson, we’re going to push that idea a lot further.
Now, we’ve already watched a machine take over derived demand once before. It happened to a job that most people have forgotten. In the early days of the telephone, every single call needed a human operator. You picked up the phone, an operator at a switchboard asked for the number, and then she plugged a cord into the right jack to connect your call.
And there’s a story about how that started to change, although it might just be a legend. There was an undertaker in Kansas City who was convinced that a telephone operator was sending his customers to a competitor. So he invented a switch that could connect calls without any operator at all. Engineering historians call it a commonly told story, which is a polite way of saying that nobody can prove it. But the switch was real, and it led to some of the first automatic telephone exchanges.
Even so, the operators didn’t disappear overnight. Phones kept spreading, and at the peak, in the late 1940s, more than 350,000 operators worked for AT&T, and almost all of them were women. Picture all of them standing shoulder to shoulder. That line of workers would stretch for about a hundred miles. Then automatic switching got better and better, and gradually, ordinary calls stopped needing an operator at all.
So ask yourself, did people stop making phone calls? Of course not. The service carried on, and the human work of connecting the call went away. Today, you tap a name on a screen, and a phone rings on the other side of the world.
And here’s the part that matters most for this course. Back when people picked up the phone and asked the operator for a number, who did they actually want to talk to? It wasn’t the operator. They wanted their mother, or their best friend, or the doctor’s office. The operator was instrumental, and the person at the other end of the line was the whole point. So inside one ordinary phone call, there were two kinds of demand. Connecting the call was derived demand, and a machine took it over. But wanting to hear that particular voice 
you were calling 
is something else entirely.
And that’s the gap that I want to leave you with. Marshall named derived demand in 1890, and its complement went without a name for 136 years. I call that complement essential demand. It covers the cases where people want a human because of the human, not only because of the result. Kojima’s name on a game is one example. The voice at the other end of the line is another. We’ll spend most of this course on essential demand. But you can’t really see it until you see how much of the economy runs on the other kind.
So let’s try a short exercise that you can do in your head right now. Think of three things that you paid for recently. For each one, finish this sentence. I paid for blank, because I wanted blank. Maybe it’s “I paid for an oil change, because I wanted my car to keep running.” Or maybe it’s “I paid for a pizza, because I wanted dinner without cooking.” Then ask the test question. If a machine could deliver that same result, just as good and just as reliable, would 
you still want the human? For most of what you buy, you’ll probably find that the answer is no. And when the answer is yes, pay attention, because that’s where this course is going.
Okay, so let’s recap the three big things from this lesson. First, derived demand means that the demand for someone’s labor comes from the demand for the result that their labor produces. That’s true for your house, your car, your refrigerator, and your TV. Second, a brand is not a person. Loving Sony means trusting a company, and caring a lot about quality doesn’t change that. And third, even skilled, personal services, like fixing the leak under your sink, can be derived demand. The telephone operators showed us that a service can carry on long after the human work behind it disappears.
Here’s how that fits into the larger argument of this course. Derived demand is the work that machines can take over, and it’s most of the work that there is. Everything after this is about the work that stays human, and why.
